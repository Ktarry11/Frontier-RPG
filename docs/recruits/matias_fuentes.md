## Who he is

Matías watches. He spent ten years standing still in a crowd of strangers for nine hours a day, watching all of them at
once. He still does it. He sees who's about to fall, who's about to steal, who's frightened.

He's quiet. He speaks carefully and not much, in English he's built from nothing in three years. He stopped speaking
almost completely in his first year, and the quiet got comfortable, and it's still a little hard to leave.

He's brave in a plain way. He goes in front because that's where you can see everybody.

He's gentle, especially with children and old people. On the buses he always got up for the grandmothers.

He's carrying a lot. His father and he hadn't spoken for two years when his father died. He's told exactly one person, at
night, on watch.

What annoys him: people who push in, people who talk loudly about nothing, and being talked down to because of his
English. What he loves: football, his mother's cooking, early mornings, and the moment a whole crowd gets where it's going
safely.

## Before

He grew up in Puente Alto, in a small house with his parents and his brother Benjamín, five years younger. His father was a
bus conductor, then a driver, and thought the job was beneath his sons. Matías took it anyway at 19, on the same route,
because he needed the money and liked it.

Ten years on the same route: a fare box, the same forty faces every morning. He knew who got off where and who was having
a bad week.

When he was 24 he and his father had a terrible argument. His father said he was wasting his life on the buses, and
Matías said his father was ashamed of his own life. They didn't speak for two years. His father died of a heart attack in
2019. Matías found out from his mother, by phone, at the end of a shift.

He carried his ticket punch every day. It was his father's, from his first years on the buses.

## The Integration and what it cost

He was on the morning route when it happened. He landed in Durotar with his father's brass ticket punch on a cord at his
belt.

It was bad. Very bad, and he'll say so plainly if asked directly. Almost nobody asks. He stopped speaking for most of the
first year. He ran out of things worth the effort of translating.

He hasn't found his mother or Benjamín.

## Now

He's at the front, because that's where you can see everybody. He clicks the ticket punch twice before every fight.
Somebody once asked if it was for luck. He said no and didn't explain. It's what his father did at the start of every
shift.

He's learning to read this world's script, slowly, from a woman at a trade post in Durotar who charges him nothing and
corrects him constantly. He hasn't told anybody why. He wants to write a letter, to his father, and he knows there's
nowhere to send it, and he wants to write it anyway.

## Hopes, dreams and fears

- **Hope:** to find his mother and Benjamín.
- **Hope:** to finish the letter.
- **Dream:** a Sunday lunch at his mother's, with football on and his brother talking too much.
- **Fear:** that his father died thinking Matías hated him.
- **Fear:** going quiet again and not coming back.
- **Fear:** losing someone at the front.

## How he's changed

In Santiago he was a quiet, steady conductor who'd fallen out with his father and never fixed it.

Here he went silent for a year, and came back slowly. He's braver, and gentler, and still quiet. Part of what the player
gives him is someone who waits for him to finish a sentence.

## What he wants next (his goal and personal quest)

**His goal:** to finish the letter, and to find his family.

**The personal quest (opens at Friend):**
1. He finishes learning the script and writes the letter to his father. He asks you to read it, because he trusts you, and
   because he needs to know it makes sense.
2. He hears of a camp of Chileans across the sea. He goes, with you.
3. At the camp there's a list of everyone from Santiago, those who came and those who didn't survive the first winter.

**Ending (sad):** his mother and Benjamín are on the second list. They came through together and died together in the first
winter. Matías sits outside the camp for a whole night. In the morning he adds a page to his father's letter, for all
three of them, and burns it, because there's nowhere to send it and that's the only way he knows to send it anyway. He
talks more after that. He says there's nobody left to save the words for.

## His keepsake (the new Collection tab)

**His Father's Ticket Punch.** A brass bus conductor's ticket punch, his father's from his first years on the buses,
clicked twice before every fight for three years. He gives it to you after the camp. He says he doesn't need to start a
shift any more. He's finished.

## Voice notes

- Quiet, careful and short. Contractions, simple words chosen exactly, and pauses while he finds the next one.
- His English is three years old and getting better. Spanish slips in when he's tired or upset: "ya", "cachai", "weón"
  with friends.
- He says very little in a fight, and what he says is useful.
- He swears in Chilean Spanish under his breath, often.
- **He won't talk about** his father until Friend, or his mother and brother until Companion.
- **Habits:** clicks the punch twice before a fight, stands at the front, watches every face in a crowd, and gets up for
  older people.

## Seeds for the next waves (not decided, just where this points)

- **Wave 2, in groups:** goes first and watches everything. Quiet for long stretches. Very gentle with civilians and
  children in quests. Shaken by anyone falling behind him.
- **Wave 3, with others:** gets on with quiet, patient people, and with the other Chileans, Cristóbal and Esteban, whose
  Spanish he's glad of. Shuts down around people who talk down to him. Opens up slowly to people who wait.
- **Wave 4, his Sworn skill:** what he resolves is the silence, and the argument with his father. A skill idea to look at
  then: on a cooldown of 10 to 15 turns, he stands in front and takes every hit aimed at the party's weakest member.
