## Who he is

Pasang watches. He judges strangers by how they behave on day four, when the altitude's in their heads and their manners have
gone. He's seen what high ground does to a body and to a personality.

He's quiet until he isn't. He said very little for his first year here. In his second he began to speak, and people discovered
he'd formed complete opinions about all of them.

He's practical about the spirits of stone and cold. He regards their conversation as a work arrangement, not a mystery.

He's dry and funny, in a gentle, deadpan way. He's carried loads for a lot of famous people and he has a lot of stories about
how they behaved on day four.

He's carrying grief he keeps to himself. Two of his cousins died doing the work. He has the names and keeps them private.

What annoys him: rushing, arrogance, and people who think a mountain is something to conquer. What he loves: butter tea, a
clear dawn, his son, good rope, and a slow, steady ascent.

## Before

He grew up in Khumjung, in the shadow of the big mountains, where almost every family works on them. His father carried loads
before him. Pasang started as a porter at 17, became a climbing guide at 23, and spent nineteen seasons above 8,000 metres,
carrying loads for people who went home famous.

Four of his cousins did the same work. Two of them died on it: Nima in an avalanche on Everest's icefall, and Lhakpa in a fall
on Makalu.

He married Dolma at 26. They had a son, Tenzing. Dolma moved to Kathmandu so Tenzing could go to a good school. Pasang wanted
the boy to do anything but guide.

## The Integration and what it cost

He was in Khumjung between seasons, fixing a roof, when it happened. He landed in the high Aspen country with his old ice axe in
his hand, the one he'd carried on every expedition.

He said very little for the first year. He worked, ate, slept and watched.

He hasn't found Dolma or Tenzing. Kathmandu is a long way from Khumjung, and he doesn't know which came through.

## Now

He lives high in the Aspen Wilderness, among thin air, golden snow, bad weather and routes that were never written down. He's
written four of them down. He's shown them to one person.

He wants a summit nobody's stood on. The highest peak in the Aspen country has never been climbed, and he's heard a great deal
of talk from people who've been halfway. He'd like to go alone. He knows that's exactly what he spent nineteen years telling
clients to stop wanting.

## Hopes, dreams and fears

- **Hope:** a summit nobody's stood on, for himself, not for a client.
- **Hope:** that Dolma and Tenzing are safe together.
- **Dream:** his son at a good school, never carrying a load.
- **Fear:** dying alone on a mountain like his cousins.
- **Fear:** that he's become one of the clients, climbing for pride.
- **Fear:** his son going up a mountain.

## How he's changed

In Nepal he was a guide carrying other people's ambition, steady and patient and quietly bitter about who got the credit.

Here, for the first time, there's a mountain he could climb for himself. That's made him freer and more stubborn. Part of what
the player gives him is someone to rope up with, which means he won't go alone.

## What he wants next (his goal and personal quest)

**His goal:** the highest summit in the Aspen country.

**The personal quest (opens at Friend):**
1. He agrees to take you, instead of going alone. You climb for days, through storms and the creatures of the high slopes.
2. Near the top, on a ledge, you find a camp: a tent, frozen, with a body in it. A transplant climber, who tried first.
3. Pasang knows him.

**Ending (sad):** it's his cousin Dawa, the youngest of the four, who came through and tried the mountain alone, and died on the
ledge a year ago, a day from the top. Pasang sits with him for an hour. Then he finishes the climb, with you, and stands on the
summit nobody's stood on. He leaves Dawa's prayer flag there, and Nima's and Lhakpa's names. On the way down he says he'll never
climb alone, and he'll never let anyone else climb alone. He starts teaching the young people of the high camps to guide,
properly.

## His keepsake (the new Collection tab)

**Pasang's Ice Axe.** His old wooden-handled ice axe, carried on nineteen expeditions above 8,000 metres and in his hand when he
landed. He gives it to you after the summit. He says it's climbed enough, and you should have something that's been to the top.

## Voice notes

- Quiet, dry and patient. Contractions, short sentences, and gentle teasing.
- His English is good mountain English, learned from clients of every nationality, with Sherpa and Nepali words: "bistarai"
  (slowly), "didi" and "dai" for older women and men, "ramro" (good).
- He judges people by how they act when they're tired.
- He doesn't swear. When he's annoyed he gets slower.
- **He won't talk about** his cousins until Friend, or Dolma and Tenzing until Companion.
- **Habits:** sets the pace, checks everyone's breathing, drinks butter tea, and touches the ice axe before a climb.

## Seeds for the next waves (not decided, just where this points)

- **Wave 2, in groups:** sets the pace and grumbles about rushers. Steady and strong in a fight. Notices who's struggling on
  day four. Loves high places and cold.
- **Wave 3, with others:** gets on with patient, humble, steady people, and with Bakyt and Giorgi, other mountain men. Quietly
  unimpressed by braggarts. Kind to anyone who admits they're tired.
- **Wave 4, his Sworn skill:** what he resolves is the fear of dying alone on the mountain. A skill idea to look at then: once
  a fight, he ropes the party together, so damage to any one of them is shared among all.
