## Who he is

Nizar rebuilds. He taught mathematics in Homs for eleven years, then in Beirut for six, then in Hamburg, in a language he was still learning, for four
more. He's rebuilt his life three times, and this is simply the fourth. He states that without self-pity, and it makes most transplants go quiet.

He's precise and courteous. His English is careful and faintly old-fashioned, learned from books and the BBC, and he's more exact in it than almost
anyone born to it.

He's patient. He explained the same proof for the ninth time without letting it show for 21 years.

He has a method for loss. The first time you lose everything it destroys you, the second time it's merely very hard, and the third time you find you
have a method. He doesn't offer this as wisdom. He offers it as information, to people who look like they need it.

He's systematic about Hala in a way that's painful to watch: hub by hub, list by list, in a notebook, with dates.

What annoys him: imprecision, people who give up on students, and pity. What he loves: an elegant proof, strong coffee with cardamom, the BBC World
Service he can no longer hear, his daughter's voice, and the moment a student understands.

## Before

He grew up in Homs, the son of a pharmacist. He married Lamia, an English teacher, at 20, and their daughter Hala was born a year later. He taught mathematics in Homs until the war made it impossible, then in Beirut, then in Hamburg, where he
learned German at fifty-hour weeks and taught in it.

Lamia died of cancer in Hamburg in 2016, before any of the rest of it. He raised Hala the rest of the way himself. She went to Amman to study
medicine at twenty. They spoke every single day, and he can tell you the time of the last call: 21:14, the night before.

His father's pocket watch came with him through every move. The crystal cracked on the boat from Beirut.

## The Integration and what it cost

He was in his Hamburg classroom, at the blackboard, when it happened. He landed in Stranglethorn with his father's watch in his waistcoat. It started
running backwards that day. He's never had it mended. He winds it every morning regardless.

He adjusted better than almost anyone, because he'd done it before and knew the shape of it.

## Now

He lives aboard the Crossing, the ship between Booty Bay and the other continent, because from it he can reach the most hubs. In a party he's at the
back, holding the order of things together while the front rank works.

He's teaching again: four children in a trade post at Booty Bay, mathematics, in the evenings, for nothing. He says it's to keep his hand in.

He keeps the notebook of hubs and lists and dates. Hala isn't in it yet.

## Hopes, dreams and fears

- **Hope:** to find Hala.
- **Hope:** that she's practising medicine somewhere, saving people.
- **Dream:** a phone call at 21:14.
- **Fear:** that the method won't work a fourth time.
- **Fear:** that she's looking for him, and they keep missing each other.
- **Fear:** the watch stopping.

## How he's changed

In three countries he was a patient, precise teacher, a widower and a devoted father, rebuilding again and again.

Here he's rebuilt a fourth time, and he's never once complained. He's kinder than he was, and more tired. Part of what the player gives him is
somebody to explain things to.

## What he wants next (his goal and personal quest)

**His goal:** to find Hala.

**The personal quest (opens at Friend):**
1. On a crossing, a Jordanian traveller mentions a young Syrian doctor who worked in the camps near the place where the Amman hub came down, and who
   kept a notebook of lists and hubs, exactly like his.
2. You go with him to the camp, through hard country.
3. The camp's healer gives him a notebook. It's Hala's.

**Ending (sad):** Hala survived the landing and spent two years as the camp's doctor, and every spare day searching for him, hub by hub, list by list,
with dates, the way he taught her. She died of a fever last winter, treating others. Her notebook has his name on every page. They were looking for
each other with the same method, and they kept missing. Nizar reads every page. He doesn't speak for a long time. Then he stops winding the watch, and
lets it run down, and it stops. He goes back to Booty Bay and teaches his four children the next morning, because Hala would have, and because he
has a method.

## His keepsake (the new Collection tab)

**Nizar's Stopped Watch.** His father's pocket watch, the crystal cracked on the boat from Beirut, which ran backwards for three years and which he
wound every morning. It's stopped now. He gives it to you after Hala's camp. He says time can go forward again, and he'd like you to keep the thing
that held it still.

## Voice notes

- Courteous, precise and gentle. Contractions, but careful, old-fashioned English: "if you would permit me", "I should like".
- He speaks Arabic, French and German as well, and slips into whichever fits.
- He explains things in sequence, like a proof.
- He doesn't swear. When something's careless he says "that is not rigorous".
- **He won't talk about** Lamia until Companion, or the time of the last call until Friend.
- **Habits:** winds the watch every morning, writes in his notebook, teaches anyone who'll listen, and stands very straight.
