## Who she is

Freja is the calmest person in any room. She spent six years being the steady one while something enormous happened
in front of her, and it shows. When a fight goes wrong her voice gets quieter, not louder.

She's blunt. She tells you what's wrong with you, what she's going to do about it, and how long it'll take. People
find it a relief almost straight away, because she's never wrong about the important things.

She's playful. Off the clock she teases everyone, she wins arguments for fun, and she laughs loudly at her own
jokes. She's competitive about everything, including things that aren't competitions. She played handball for twelve
years, and she'll still tell you it's a better sport than whatever you play.

She's strong, in her body and in her head. She doesn't back down. Not to a bully, not to an officer, and not to anyone
who thinks the Light makes them special. She's met two people here who decided they were chosen, and she told both of
them to their faces what she thought of that.

She's warm underneath all of it, and it comes out in what she does more than what she says. She remembers how
everyone takes their tea. She stays up with the ones who can't sleep. She'd never call it kindness. She'd call it
looking after her patients.

What annoys her: panicking, people who think they're above anyone, people who won't do as they're told when they're
bleeding, and being treated like she's delicate because she's a healer. What she loves: a good argument, a job done
properly, people who give it back as good as they get, and William.

## Before

She grew up in Aarhus. Her father was a ferry engineer and her mother ran a flower shop. Her grandfather came over from
Sweden as a young man to work at the shipyard, which is where the Swedish name comes from. She has one older brother,
Mads, who lives in Copenhagen and phones on birthdays.

She was the girl who fought boys at school and won. She played handball from eight to twenty, on the pivot, which is
the position where you take the most hits. She trained as a midwife at 20 because she wanted a job where she'd be
needed at the worst moment and wouldn't be allowed to panic.

She worked six years at the university hospital, mostly nights. She had a bicycle she rode in every weather, a flat
with far too many plants, and a reputation on the ward for being the one the frightened mothers asked for.

She met William when she was 24. He was an American aircraft mechanic who'd taken a job keeping planes running at
Billund airport, an hour from Aarhus. He came off his bicycle on an icy road outside the hospital, because Americans
can't ride bicycles, and she was the one walking past at the end of her shift. She strapped his wrist on the pavement
and told him Americans shouldn't be allowed bicycles. He told her Danes shouldn't be allowed opinions. He asked her
out before she'd finished the bandage.

William is from Washington, sarcastic down to the bone, and he can't stand stupid people. He's also the most loyal man
she's ever met. He'd do anything for the people he loves, and he'd complain the whole time he was doing it. They argued
about everything, they laughed through most of it, and they married when she was 26. She kept her own name. He said
it was the first sensible thing a Dane had ever done.

They talked about children. They were going to start trying the next spring.

## The Integration and what it cost

When the worlds joined she was finishing a night shift in Aarhus. William was at work at Billund. She landed in the
Valley of Trials still in her hospital clothes, with her pinard horn in her pocket. That's the wooden trumpet midwives
use to listen for a baby's heartbeat.

She didn't cry. She found the infirmary, asked who was in charge, and started work. There were a lot of hurt people in
those first weeks, and she used that work the way you use a railing on a staircase. She knows exactly what she was
doing. She'd do it again, and she wouldn't recommend it to anybody.

She's checked the Danish lists at two hubs. Neither one is complete. William isn't on them, and neither are her
parents. William wouldn't be on a Danish list anyway, and she doesn't know if anyone's keeping an American one. She's
decided to treat not knowing as good news for as long as she can stand to.

The playfulness went first, in the first month. It came back slowly, mostly through arguing with people.

## Now

She walks a circuit of the farms and camps around the Valley, because the fighting always gets a healer and the births
get whoever happens to be nearest.

She's taught four women to do what she does when she isn't there. That's the part of the work she's proudest of, and
it's the part nobody ever asks her about.

The Light came to her easily. She treats it as a tool, the same as her hands and her horn, and she's careful to say so.

She writes William a letter every week and keeps them in a bundle. She doesn't send them, because there's nowhere to
send them. She's going to make him read every one.

## Hopes, dreams and fears

- **Hope:** that William is alive and somewhere he's annoying people.
- **Hope:** that her parents are together, wherever they are.
- **Dream:** to deliver a baby here. Six years of her life were about the first ninety seconds of somebody else's. She
  wants to know if that still works in a place with this much dying in it. She's told three people and none of them
  understood.
- **Dream:** the children she and William were going to have. She doesn't talk about that one at all.
- **Fear:** freezing at the wrong moment. It's never happened. She thinks about it every time.
- **Fear:** finding William and finding out he's different.
- **Fear:** that she'll stop being able to treat not knowing as good news.

## How she's changed

In Aarhus she had a settled life: calm at work, loud at home, a husband to argue with and a plan for the spring.

Here she's harder. She's seen more death in three years than in six on the ward, and she's learned to keep going
through it. She's more protective of people, quicker to fight, and less patient with nonsense.

The joking came back, and it's sharper now. She uses it to keep people steady, herself included. Part of what the
player gives her is somebody to argue with again, which she's missed more than she'll admit.

## Her keepsake (the new Collection tab)

**Freja's Pinard Horn.** The wooden midwife's horn she had in her pocket on the night of the Integration, polished
dark from years of use. She listened to the goblin baby's heartbeat with it. William carves her a new one from wood
here, so she gives the old one to you. She says you've kept her alive long enough to earn it.

## Voice notes

- Short, plain and direct. Contractions, the useful thing first, and instructions given like a nurse gives them.
- Her English is excellent and very Danish: direct to the point of rude, with no softening words. She doesn't say
  "maybe" when she means "no".
- She teases people she likes and is coldly polite to people she doesn't. If she's polite to you, worry.
- She swears in Danish when something hurts, usually "for helvede".
- She calls William "my idiot" and means it as the nicest thing she can say.
- **She won't talk about** her parents until Companion, the children they planned until Sworn, or how scared she is for
  William at any stage. She'll joke about him instead.
- **Habits:** checks everyone's colour after a fight, cracks her knuckles before she heals, keeps a running score of
  arguments she's won, and taps the horn in her pocket when she's thinking.
